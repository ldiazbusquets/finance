About This Site

An independent finance site by Luis Diaz Busquets, a finance student at Bryant University with minors in Applied AI and Law.

Why it exists

Most research hides its assumptions. Here, every number links back to where it came from and every model shows its inputs, so you can disagree with a conclusion and see exactly where.

The site sits alongside the main site, which covers who I am and my experience, and LDB Lab, which is about machine learning.

Objectivity

How Ratings Work

Every public company gets a Buy, Hold or Sell from the same rule, whoever owns it. The rule starts from the report’s expected value (below) and the price at writing.

Because the value depends on the discount rate, the same assumptions are first applied to the whole S&P 500: a 5.15% 10-year Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity risk premium. On those assumptions the index is worth about 68% of its price, so part of every stock’s gap is the method, not the stock. The rating compares each stock with the market on that same basis:

  • Buy: at least 15% cheaper than the market.
  • Hold: within 15% either way.
  • Sell: at least 15% dearer than the market.

Relative, not absolute. A Buy means cheaper than the market on the same assumptions, not necessarily cheaper than its own value. So each rating also carries an absolute label, based on the expected value against the price: undervalued (value at least 10% above the price), near fair value (within 10%) or overvalued (value at least 10% below). A stock can be a relative Buy and still overvalued on its own, when the whole market is.

Ranges, not three cases. Three cases weighted 25/50/25 is a convention, not a measured probability. So each report’s own model is rerun 4,000 times with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle of each range, and the bear and bull cases are its 10th and 90th percentiles, so each side can be a different width. Growth, margins, multiples and capital spending share a common factor (correlation 0.6), because they tend to go well or badly together; the discount rate varies on its own. The expected value is the average of the 4,000 results, and it is what the rating uses. Each report also shows the likely range (10th to 90th percentile), the chance its value beats the price, and the chance it is cheaper than the market. The draws are seeded, so the numbers only change when a model does. The code is montecarlo.py.

Each report shows both numbers, the absolute value and the comparison with the market, and says whether the rating would change if the index grew 6% or 10% a year instead of 8%. Private companies are not rated, since there is no price to buy them at; the Costco report is university coursework and is left as written.

Other rules
Ownership
Stated once, in each report’s disclosure line. It never enters the rating or the recommendation text.
Sources
Company releases and SEC filings first; news sites only for prices, analyst targets and reactions. Figures that are the analyst’s own estimates are marked as such.
Changes
When a rating changes, the old one stays in the track record, which scores every call against the S&P 500.
Known lean
On these assumptions the models sit below analysts’ targets almost everywhere. Each report explains its own gap with the Street.

Everything on this site is for education. It is not investment advice, and nothing here is a recommendation to buy or sell any security.

Verification

That cannot catch a figure copied wrong from a filing, so reports are also checked by hand against the primary source: the company’s own release or SEC filing, never a news summary.
