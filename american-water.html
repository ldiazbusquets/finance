American Water

01 · The business

Regulated pipes, regulated returns.

American Water supplies drinking water and wastewater service in 14 states and to military bases. State regulators set its prices to allow a fair return on the money it invests (its rate base), so profits grow as it replaces old pipes and treatment plants.

In October 2025 it agreed an all-stock merger with Essential Utilities (0.305 American Water shares per Essential share; about 69/31 ownership), creating a company with roughly $29 billion of rate base. Closing is expected by the end of the first quarter of 2027.

04 · Balance sheet

Debt-funded growth, by design.

Debt is about $16 billion against $11.7 billion of equity. The company sold forward 8.1 million shares in 2025 at about $140 and still has 4.7 million to settle by year-end, so modest dilution is part of the plan.

07 · Risks

What could go wrong, and what could go right.

RiskWhy it matters
Interest ratesUtility shares move inversely to bond yields; higher yields mean lower prices.

Upside: falling interest rates would lift the value quickly: at a 7% cost of equity the model gives $139.

08 · Rating

Hold. A reliable business with a rate-sensitive price.

American Water’s earnings are among the most predictable in the market, but the price implies a cost of equity well below what today’s Treasury yields justify.

What would change the value: Lower long-term interest rates, or rate-case outcomes that lift allowed returns, would raise the value; higher yields would lower it.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $101. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades above our estimated value, which is 28% below the price.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating depends on that assumption: it would be Buy if the index grows 6% a year and Hold at 10%, against 8% here. How ratings work
