Amazon

Amazon is three businesses in one: the largest online store, the largest cloud (AWS) and a fast-growing advertising arm. AWS growth sped up to 39% in the third quarter, its fastest in almost five years, but Amazon said spending will rise again in 2027 from about $220 billion this year, and it now borrows to pay for it. 01 · The business

A store, a cloud and an ad network.

Amazon reports three segments. North America and International are the store: first-party retail, third-party seller services, Prime subscriptions and advertising. AWS rents computing, storage and AI infrastructure to companies and governments.

Most of the profit comes from AWS and advertising, which ride on the store’s traffic. Retail itself earns thin margins but funds the logistics network that keeps customers loyal. Amazon also owns a large stake in Anthropic, the AI company, which it does not value in its operating results.

Takeaway. Value comes mostly from AWS and ads; the store is the moat that feeds them.

02 · Latest results

AWS is growing 39% on a $184 billion pace.

Third-quarter revenue rose 12% to $201.9 billion, slightly below the $202.3 billion analysts expected; moving Prime Day into the second quarter took about four points off growth. AWS revenue grew 39% to $46.0 billion with $17.8 billion of operating income, and its backlog reached $578 billion. Operating income of $28.1 billion, a record 13.9% margin, beat the $22.5–26.5 billion guidance; North America earned 7.7% on sales and International 3.7%. Advertising grew 11% to $19.6 billion. EPS of $1.89 was below the $1.96 expected.

The cost is in cash flow: free cash flow over the last twelve months was negative $42.4 billion as Amazon defines it, because capital spending (net of proceeds) rose 82% in a year, to $211 billion. Capital spending is still guided to about $220 billion for 2026, and management said it will “rise meaningfully” in 2027. Long-term debt has risen to $173 billion from $66 billion at the end of 2025. Guidance for the fourth quarter is $240–247 billion of revenue and $28.5–33.5 billion of operating income. The shares, which closed at $243.79 before the report, fell about 6% in after-hours trading.

04 · Balance sheet

Strong, but now borrowing to build.

Amazon held about $114 billion of cash and marketable securities at the end of September against $173 billion of long-term debt, so net debt is now about $59 billion, up from roughly $10 billion in June. It pays no dividend and buys back little; every spare dollar goes into data centers, chips (Trainium) and the Leo satellite network. The Anthropic stake is a sizeable asset that is not included in this valuation, so it is upside to the numbers below; Anthropic’s planned IPO would give it a market price.

06 · What the price assumes

At $244, the build-out has to earn a lot.

To reach today’s price, you needInstead of
A long-run operating margin near 26%18%; Amazon’s best quarter so far was 13.9%
Revenue growth of about 22% a year for two years13% and 12%
A discount rate of about 8.3%10.7%

The market is betting that the $220 billion a year going into AI infrastructure, and more in 2027, earns AWS-like returns. The Anthropic stake, left out of the model, covers part of the gap.

08 · Rating

Hold. A great company in an expensive building phase.

AWS growth accelerated to 39% in the third quarter, but capital spending has turned free cash flow deeply negative and will rise again in 2027. The price assumes long-run margins higher than Amazon has ever earned.

What would change the value: The size of the 2027 capital spending plan, AWS growth staying near 40%, and operating income against the fourth-quarter guidance would move the value.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $164. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades above our estimated value, which is 36% below the price.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating is the same whether the index is assumed to grow 6% or 10% a year. How ratings work

Own the platform, wait for the cash.

The spending is a bet on AI that only pays off if AWS keeps its margins as it grows.
