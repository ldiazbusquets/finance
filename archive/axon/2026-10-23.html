01 · The business

Devices that feed a software subscription.

Axon sells two things. Connected Devices (56% of revenue) are TASER energy weapons, Axon Body cameras, in-car and fixed cameras, drones and, since buying Dedrone in 2024, counter-drone systems. Software & Services (44%) is Axon Evidence, the cloud where agencies store and share footage, plus records, dispatch and AI tools. Devices are sold inside five- to ten-year bundles, so about 95% of revenue is tied to subscription plans and customers rarely switch.

The AI Era Plan, an add-on of about $199 per officer a year, includes Draft One (police reports written from camera audio), transcription and real-time translation. Revenue from it grew almost 700% in the past year.

Second-quarter revenue beat estimates by about $28 million. TASER grew 21%, and Platform Solutions (drones, counter-drone, fixed cameras) more than doubled, with Dedrone passing $100 million in the quarter. Management raised 2026 growth guidance to 32–34% and set 2028 targets of $6 billion of revenue and a 28% adjusted EBITDA margin.

The shares fell anyway. The software gross margin dropped about four points to 71.3% as services and new products grew, memory chips raised camera costs, and free cash flow was roughly zero. In September Axon sold $1 billion of 0% convertible notes, which investors read as a sign of dilution ahead. Results for the third quarter come on November 3.

04 · The translation thesis

Can AI translation be a game changer?

Real-time translation already ships. Through Axon Assistant, an officer presses a button on an Axon Body 4 or Body Mini camera, picks a language (or lets it detect one), and the camera speaks the translation aloud; the reply is translated back into English. It covers more than 50 languages and became generally available on August 1, 2025. Frederick, Maryland, ran 1,027 translated conversations in five weeks of testing; officers said quality varied by language, and certified interpreters are still used for investigations.

The economics are what matter for the stock. Translation is sold inside the AI Era Plan rather than as a separate device, so its value shows up as higher revenue per officer on a camera base Axon already owns. In the U.S. about one in five people speaks a language other than English at home, so the feature is useful on almost every shift, which helps agencies justify the upgrade.

Military and federal use is a real but early opportunity. Federal agencies added more than 22,000 body cameras and evidence licenses in 2026, the VA bought TASER 10s, and in October DHS selected Dedrone for counter-drone protection. Axon does not report military revenue separately, and armed forces have their own translation programs and suppliers, so this report treats military translation as upside rather than part of the base case.

Takeaway. Translation is a strong reason to upgrade, but it is a feature Axon sells to customers it already has, not a new product line. It supports the 2028 targets more than it changes them.

08 · Rating

Buy. The right thesis, most of it already in the price.

Axon has a near-monopoly in U.S. police cameras, sticky subscriptions and a fast-growing AI product line. The price assumes growth beyond the company’s own targets.

What would change the value: Third-quarter results on November 3, especially margins after stock pay, are the next test.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $335. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades above our estimated value, which is 16% below the price.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating depends on that assumption: it would be Buy if the index grows 6% a year and Hold at 10%, against 8% here. How ratings work
