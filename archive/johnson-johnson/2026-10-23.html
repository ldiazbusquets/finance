01 · The business

Drugs and devices.

Innovative Medicine (about two-thirds of sales) sells cancer drugs such as Darzalex and Carvykti, immunology drugs such as Tremfya, and neuroscience drugs such as Spravato. MedTech sells heart devices, surgical tools, orthopaedics and contact lenses. The consumer business was spun off as Kenvue in 2023.

The challenge is replacing Stelara, its top seller, which lost U.S. patent protection in 2025. New drugs have so far more than made up for it.

Third-quarter sales rose 5.9% to $25.4 billion. Innovative Medicine grew 7.7% even though Stelara’s decline took about 6.9 points off its growth; Tremfya, Darzalex, Carvykti, Rybrevant and Spravato led. MedTech grew 2.5%. 04 · Balance sheet and ownership

AAA-rated, buying pipeline.

At September 27 J&J had $44.7 billion of debt and $17.2 billion of cash, about $27 billion of net debt; this model also deducts the proposed $5.5 billion talc settlement. It is one of two U.S. companies with a AAA credit rating and has raised its dividend every year for six decades. It buys much of its pipeline: free cash flow of about $15 billion so far this year is shared between dividends and acquisitions.

08 · Rating

Hold. Through the Stelara cliff, priced like a bond.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $198. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades above our estimated value, which is 28% below the price.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating depends on that assumption: it would be Buy if the index grows 6% a year and Hold at 10%, against 8% here. How ratings work
