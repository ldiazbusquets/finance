01 · The business

Missiles, engines and aircraft parts.

Raytheon makes Patriot air defense, the Standard Missile family, AMRAAM, Tomahawk and the Coyote counter-drone interceptor. Pratt & Whitney makes the GTF engine for the Airbus A320neo family and the F135 for the F-35. Collins Aerospace makes avionics, seats, landing gear and other parts for airliners and military aircraft.

About half of sales are commercial. That makes RTX the prime most tied to airline traffic and aftermarket parts, and the least dependent on U.S. defense budgets alone.

03 · The Anduril question

Cheap missiles against expensive ones.

Anduril, valued at about $90 billion this month, sells exactly the kind of low-cost weapons that sit next to Raytheon’s: the Roadrunner reusable interceptor against drones, and the Barracuda low-cost cruise missile. Raytheon’s own Coyote interceptor competes in the same counter-drone market. The high end, Patriot, Standard Missile and AMRAAM, is protected by years of qualification and some of the largest multi-year production deals the Pentagon has signed.

05 · Balance sheet and ownership

Debt falling, cash flow rising.

At the end of September RTX had $9.2 billion of cash and $37.4 billion of debt, about $28.2 billion of net debt, down from about $30 billion at the start of the year. There are about 1.37 billion diluted shares. Free cash flow guidance of about $9.1 billion this year is roughly 9% of sales.

09 · Rating

Hold. Half aerospace, half missiles, a record backlog.

RTX has a record $340 billion backlog, raised guidance again in October, and gets about half its sales from commercial aerospace, which no defense start-up competes in. Where Anduril does compete, in cheap interceptors and cruise missiles, it is new spending at stake rather than the backlog.

What would change the value: January’s 2027 guidance, munitions contract awards and the pace of GTF engine inspections are the next inputs; the midterms on November 3 could change the budget outlook.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $152. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades above our estimated value, which is 24% below the price.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating depends on that assumption: it would be Buy if the index grows 6% a year and Hold at 10%, against 8% here. How ratings work
