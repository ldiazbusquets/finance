01 · The business

The world’s chip factory.

TSMC does not design chips; it manufactures them for the companies that do. It makes nearly all of the most advanced ones: Nvidia’s AI accelerators, Apple’s processors, AMD’s CPUs and GPUs, and the custom AI chips Google, Amazon and others design. It also packages them together with memory (CoWoS), which has been the tightest bottleneck in AI hardware.

Its edge is manufacturing at the leading edge, now the 2-nanometer (N2) process, at a scale and yield no rival matches. Most production is in Taiwan, with new fabs in Arizona, Japan and Germany.

Third-quarter revenue rose 41% in dollars to $46.7 billion and profit about 69%. The gross margin of 66.4% slipped 1.3 points from the second quarter as the 2-nanometer ramp diluted it. N2 was 8% of wafer revenue, and its capacity should more than double in 2027.

TSMC guided fourth-quarter revenue to $52.3–54.3 billion and expects about 44% growth for 2026. Management said its forecast of mid-to-high-50s% annual growth in AI accelerator revenue through 2029 will “probably” be higher, but warned that overseas fabs will dilute the gross margin by 3–4 points in later years. Capital spending of $60–64 billion this year will rise “even more significantly” in 2026–2028.

04 · Balance sheet and ownership

Cash-rich, spending more each year.

TSMC holds far more cash than debt; this model uses net cash of about $60 billion (an estimate from its latest balance sheet). It pays a quarterly dividend it intends to keep raising. There are about 25.9 billion Taiwan shares, or 5.19 billion ADR-equivalents.

07 · Risks

What could go wrong, and what could go right.

RiskWhy it matters
TaiwanMost capacity is in Taiwan; any conflict or blockade would be the largest single risk to the chip industry.
The chip cycleCapacity booms have always been followed by downturns; capex is rising sharply.
Overseas costsArizona, Japan and Germany fabs cost more to run and dilute margins.
Customer concentrationApple and Nvidia are a large share of revenue.
Tariffs and export rulesU.S. chip tariffs and China export limits could change where and for whom it builds.

Upside: AI accelerator growth above management’s forecast, N2 pricing, advanced packaging capacity, and a stronger Taiwan dollar lifting dollar results.

08 · Rating

Buy. The factory every AI chip needs, priced for its own forecast.

TSMC makes almost every advanced chip and is growing about 44% this year at a 66% gross margin. The price matches the model’s base case, which follows management’s own outlook. The concentration of production in Taiwan is a risk no model captures well.

What would change the value: Fourth-quarter results in January with 2027 guidance, N2 pricing and the size of the 2027 capex increase are the next inputs.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $412. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades within 10% of our estimated value.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating is the same whether the index is assumed to grow 6% or 10% a year. How ratings work

Against the Street: Analysts average about $529, assuming growth stays above 25% for longer. The base case here follows TSMC’s own forecast and fades growth from 2029.
