01 · The business

A toll road for card payments.

Visa does not lend money or issue cards. Banks issue Visa cards, and Visa charges a small fee on each payment that runs over its network, plus more for cross-border payments, currency conversion and services such as fraud tools. Costs barely rise with volume, so most extra revenue becomes profit.

Growth comes from cash and checks moving to cards, travel abroad, and newer flows such as payments between businesses and to individuals.

04 · Balance sheet and ownership

Almost all profit returned.

At June 30 Visa had $23.9 billion of debt and $13.9 billion of cash and investments, about $10 billion of net debt. It returns nearly all of its free cash flow through buybacks and a dividend of $0.67 a quarter, and has about 1.90 billion diluted shares.

08 · Rating

Hold. The best toll road in finance, at a record price.

Visa grows double digits at a margin above 60% with little capital. The price, at a record, needs growth of about 18% a year or a required return near 7%; on the same rule as every other company, that is close to the market, not cheaper.

What would change the value: Fiscal fourth-quarter results and the 2027 outlook on October 27, and any U.S. card-fee legislation, are the next inputs.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $294. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades above our estimated value, which is 30% below the price.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating is the same whether the index is assumed to grow 6% or 10% a year. How ratings work

Against the Street: Analysts average about $426, valuing Visa on about 30 times earnings. This model uses an 8.7% discount rate; at about 7% it matches the price.
