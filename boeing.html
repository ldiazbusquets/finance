01 · The business

Half of the world’s big-jet duopoly.

Boeing Commercial Airplanes builds the 737, 767, 777 and 787; Defense, Space & Security builds military aircraft, satellites and weapons; Global Services supports both fleets with parts and maintenance, its most profitable unit. With Airbus it shares a duopoly in large passenger jets, so demand is not the problem: production is.

Boeing delivered 171 aircraft in the second quarter, including 129 737 MAXs, and free cash flow turned positive at $631 million. It still reported a core loss, including a $280 million charge on the Air Force One program. The 737 is ramping toward 47 a month, with a new line in Everett and a next step to 52; the 787 is at 8 a month, held back by engine supply; the 777X remains on track for first delivery in 2027.

04 · Balance sheet

Heavy debt, falling.

Debt is about $45.6 billion against $20.0 billion of cash in June, down from over $50 billion. Shareholders’ equity only recently turned positive after years of losses. The 2024 equity raise and asset sales were used to protect the investment-grade rating.

06 · What the price assumes

At $190, about $11 billion of cash by 2030.

The price implies free cash flow of roughly $11 billion a year by 2030, almost exactly the base case and close to the level Boeing reached before 2019. The market is pricing in a successful recovery, but not more than that.

08 · Rating

Buy. The recovery is priced in. Execution decides the rest.

Boeing’s backlog guarantees demand; the value depends on building planes at the planned rate and quality.

What would change the value: Monthly 737 and 787 production rates, FAA decisions and free cash flow against guidance are the inputs to watch.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $197. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades within 10% of our estimated value.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating is the same whether the index is assumed to grow 6% or 10% a year. How ratings work
