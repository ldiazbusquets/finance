Boeing

01 · The business

Half of the world’s big-jet duopoly.

Boeing Commercial Airplanes builds the 737, 767, 777 and 787; Defense, Space & Security builds military aircraft, satellites and weapons; Global Services supports both fleets with parts and maintenance, its most profitable unit. With Airbus it shares a duopoly in large passenger jets, so demand is not the problem: production is.

Boeing delivered 171 aircraft in the second quarter, including 129 737 MAXs, and free cash flow turned positive at $631 million. It still reported a core loss, including a $280 million charge on the Air Force One program. The 737 is ramping toward 47 a month, with a new line in Everett and a next step to 52; the 787 is at 8 a month, held back by engine supply; the 777X remains on track for first delivery in 2027.

04 · Balance sheet

Heavy debt, falling.

Debt is about $45.6 billion against $20.0 billion of cash in June, down from over $50 billion. Shareholders’ equity only recently turned positive after years of losses. The 2024 equity raise and asset sales were used to protect the investment-grade rating.

06 · What the price assumes

At $190, about $11 billion of cash by 2030.

The price implies free cash flow of roughly $11 billion a year by 2030, almost exactly the base case and close to the level Boeing reached before 2019. The market is pricing in a successful recovery, but not more than that.

08 · Recommendation

Hold. Fairly priced for the recovery.

At $190.47 against a base case of $197, Boeing is roughly fairly valued: the price assumes the turnaround works, no more. The call is Hold. The upside depends on beating the plan; a stumble on quality or production would hit the shares hard.

Final view

The recovery is priced in. Execution decides the rest.

Demand is guaranteed by the backlog; Boeing has to build the planes.
