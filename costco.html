01 · The business

A membership business that happens to sell groceries.

Costco runs a membership-based warehouse model: a limited selection of branded and private-label goods at low prices, for households and small businesses. The strategy is high volume, fast inventory turnover and tight cost control, which keeps merchandise margins thin. A large share of operating income comes from annual membership fees, a stable and recurring stream, and the Kirkland Signature private label adds loyalty and margin.

“To continually provide our members with quality goods and services at the lowest possible prices.”Costco’s mission

Sector

Consumer Staples, Retail Consumer Staples subsector, as a warehouse club. Demand for food, household goods and everyday necessities holds up across the cycle, which gives Costco steady traffic and predictable cash flow.

Industry outlook

Staples retail is defensive, and scale wins on supplier terms and logistics. The pressures are labor costs, supply-chain inflation, thin margins and the cost of building e-commerce. Disciplined, loyal-customer businesses like Costco are best placed.

How it reaches members

  • Warehouses are the main channel: bulk buying at low margins.
  • Costco.com adds large and specialty items.
  • Services such as pharmacy, optical, hearing aids, fuel, food courts and travel bring members back.
  • Business sales serve small and mid-sized companies with bulk purchasing and delivery.

No single supplier or customer is a material share of purchases or revenue, so concentration risk is low.

02 · Profitability

The lowest margins in the group, and the steadiest returns.

From fiscal 2021 to 2025, gross margin sat around 12 to 13% every year, the lowest of the four, by design. Even so, operating margin rose from 3.47% to 3.77% and net margin from 2.56% to 2.94%, a steady gain in efficiency. Target’s margins swung sharply in 2022, Walmart’s were stable and moderate, and BJ’s ran slightly above Costco without improving as consistently.

Returns are where Costco pulls away: return on assets climbed from 8.45% to about 10.5%, and return on net operating assets held at roughly 30 to 33%.

03 · Revenue quality

Growth that comes from scale, not from markup.

72.7%Revenue from the U.S.

Top-line growth comes from net merchandise sales and membership fees: new warehouses, comparable-sales growth and high renewal rates. Cost of goods stays at 87 to 88% of revenue, so growth is coming from volume and efficiency, not wider margins. BJ’s cost of goods runs about 81 to 82%, a different pricing structure, with growth that has slowed more noticeably.

SG&A held near 9% of revenue through inflation and wage pressure, against 14 to 15% at BJ’s, and operating income as a share of revenue is flat to rising: operating leverage as the business scales.

Earnings quality is high. Operating cash flow has exceeded net income every year, so reported profit is backed by cash rather than accruals. BJ’s also clears 1.0 but with more swings from working capital.

Costco is a pure-play warehouse club. About 86 to 87% of revenue comes from the Americas and roughly 92% from developed markets, which limits political and currency risk but also limits exposure to faster-growing emerging markets.

Customers pay before suppliers are due.

05 · Debt

Almost no leverage, interest covered eighty times.

Costco carries far less debt than BJ’s, Target or Walmart. In 2025 interest-bearing debt was about 2.46% of the market value of equity and 12.89% of total assets, the lowest of the group. EBITDA covers interest more than 80 times.

The Altman Z-score stayed above the safe-zone line of 3 every year, carried by a market value far above total liabilities and more than three dollars of sales per dollar of assets. The liquidity term looks weak only because working capital is negative, which is normal for a fast-turning retailer.

Long-term debt is mostly senior unsecured notes plus leases on warehouses and offices, with maturities spread over many years to keep refinancing risk low.

06 · Cost of capital

A discount rate of 8.36%, built with CAPM.

8.36%WACC, base case
4.22%Risk-free rate, 10-year Treasury
5.0%Market risk premium
0.86Beta, FactSet and Capital IQ average

The weighted average cost of capital blends the cost of debt and the cost of equity, and it is the discount rate in the valuation. Cost of equity comes from the Capital Asset Pricing Model: a 4.22% risk-free rate, a 5.0% market risk premium and a beta of 0.86, the average of the FactSet and S&P Capital IQ estimates. Cost of debt is from market data, after tax, with weights from the market value of equity and the book value of debt.

A lower beta lowers the cost of equity and the WACC; a higher one raises both. With debt so small, the WACC is almost entirely the cost of equity.

08 · Peer multiples

Peers say $300 to $500. That is the premium, measured.

Per-share figures for Costco and its peers come from forward FactSet estimates, so every comparison is on the same basis. Costco trades at a premium on every multiple, most of all on price to earnings and price to cash flow.

Peer multiples were summarized by average, first quartile and third quartile. Given Costco’s quality, the third quartile was used as the benchmark, and even that implies only about $300 to $500 a share.

Insight. The premium is large enough that ordinary peer multiples cannot capture it. The market is paying for the membership model and its consistency, not for this year’s earnings.

09 · Recommendation

Hold and wait. A great company with no margin of safety.

At $970.31 against a value of $831.50, the stock implies about 14.31% downside. The fundamentals are excellent, but the price leaves no room for error, so the call is Hold / Wait for a better entry point.

Why the cash-flow model carries all the weight

Multiples (P/E, P/S, P/B and P/CF) give roughly $395 to $500 a share. They were left out of the final number because they cannot capture the membership revenue and the stability of long-run growth, so the cash-flow model has a 100% weight.

Where this differs from the Street

Analysts are more optimistic: an average target of about $1,072 within a range of $650 to $1,315, with Buy and Overweight ratings at roughly 55 to 65% over time and very few Sells. Their targets build in growth, continued dominance and premium multiples. This analysis puts price discipline first: even the best companies can be poor investments at the wrong price.

Final view

Own the business, wait for the price.

Costco remains a high-quality, well-managed company with strong long-term fundamentals. Its valuation is what limits the upside.
