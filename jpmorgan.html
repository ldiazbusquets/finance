01 · The business

Four businesses, one balance sheet.

JPMorgan runs Consumer & Community Banking (Chase branches, cards, mortgages), the Commercial & Investment Bank (lending, investment banking, trading, payments), and Asset & Wealth Management. Deposits are its raw material: cheap, sticky funding it lends and invests at a spread.

Scale is the advantage. It spends more on technology than most competitors earn, and it is the default counterparty for large companies and governments.

Takeaway. A bank is valued on what it earns on its equity, not on cash flow, because debt is its product.

Third-quarter net income was $15.3 billion. Net interest income rose 13% to $27.1 billion; markets revenue rose 15% to $10.3 billion, led by equities (+49%); investment banking fees rose 14%. Expenses grew 12%. Credit was steady: net charge-offs fell to $2.2 billion.

08 · Rating

Buy. The best bank, valued on its returns.

JPMorgan earns more on its equity than its peers, and the excess-return model capitalises that. The price needs returns above the bank’s own through-cycle target.

What would change the value: A change to the 16% through-cycle return target, credit losses rising faster than expected, or a move in rates that changes the cost of equity would change the value.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $290. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades above our estimated value, which is 14% below the price.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating depends on that assumption: it would be Buy if the index grows 6% a year and Hold at 10%, against 8% here. How ratings work
