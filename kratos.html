01 · The business

Cheap drones, rockets and engines.

Kratos, based in San Diego, sells to the Pentagon in two segments. Government Solutions (about 83% of revenue) includes hypersonic and rocket systems such as Zeus and Erinyes, small turbojet and turbofan engines for missiles and drones, microwave electronics, and satellite ground networks. Unmanned Systems builds target drones and the XQ-58 Valkyrie, a low-cost jet drone designed to fly alongside crewed fighters, which the Marine Corps is buying.

Its strategy is the same as Anduril’s: spend its own money to build cheap, “attritable” weapons before the government asks for them. The difference is that Kratos is public, so you can see the cost of that strategy every quarter.

04 · Balance sheet and ownership

$1.4 billion of cash, paid for with new shares.

Kratos held $1.44 billion of cash in June and almost no borrowings beyond about $140 million of finance leases, after raising roughly $1 billion by selling stock earlier in 2026. The share count is about 188 million. The cash covers the factory build-out, but stock-based pay of about $60 million a year keeps adding shares.

08 · Rating

Hold. The same idea as Anduril, at a fraction of the margin.

Kratos has many of the same products as Anduril and is growing, but it earns very little today, and the price assumes revenue far above its own plan.

What would change the value: Rising margins in the 2027 guidance, or a larger production contract for its drones, would raise the value.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $26. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades above our estimated value, which is 32% below the price.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating is the same whether the index is assumed to grow 6% or 10% a year. How ratings work

Against the Street: Analysts average about $98, assuming faster growth and margins well above the 9% base case here. Kratos has never earned more than a low single-digit operating margin.
