01 · The business

The default supplier of AI computing.

Nvidia designs graphics processors and the systems built around them: full racks of GPUs, CPUs and networking that data centers buy as one unit, plus CUDA, the software layer most AI developers write for. It does not own factories; TSMC makes its chips.

Data centers are now almost the whole company: $89.0 billion of $96.2 billion in revenue last quarter. Its biggest customers are the cloud companies (Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Oracle, Meta) and a fast-growing group of specialist AI clouds. Several of those customers also design their own chips, which is the main long-run competitive threat alongside AMD.

Takeaway. The lead rests on three things competitors have to match at once: the chip, the networking that ties thousands of them together, and the software developers already use.

In the quarter to July, revenue rose 18% from the previous quarter and 106% from a year earlier. Operating income was $63.7 billion, about two-thirds of revenue. Management guided the next quarter to $108 billion, assuming no data-center sales to China, with gross margin of about 74%.

The product cycle is moving to Vera Rubin, which the company says is in full production and ramping at cloud partners. Nvidia also returned about $26 billion through buybacks and dividends in the quarter and has about $99 billion left under its buyback authorization.

04 · Balance sheet

Plenty of cash, returned through buybacks.

Debt is small next to a $5 trillion market value, and cash and investments roughly cover it, so the model ignores net debt. Almost all cash returns come through buybacks; the dividend is token. The bigger balance-sheet items are supply commitments: Nvidia pre-pays and commits to suppliers to secure chip and memory capacity, which would become a burden if demand fell quickly.

Read. No financial risk; the exposure is operational, through supply commitments made in a boom.

06 · What the price assumes

At $216, the market prices in a longer boom.

To reach today’s price, you needInstead of
Revenue growth of about 84% next year, then 15%40%, then 15%
A long-run operating margin near 73%55%

None of these is impossible for Nvidia; each has been beaten before. But the price needs the AI build-out to keep growing well past 2027 without customers’ own chips taking meaningful share.

07 · Risks

What could go wrong, and what could go right.

RiskWhy it matters
Customer spending slowsA handful of cloud companies buy most of the output. If their returns on AI disappoint, orders can fall quickly.
Custom chipsGoogle, Amazon, Microsoft and Meta all design their own accelerators and would rather not pay Nvidia’s margins.
China and export rulesGuidance assumes no data-center sales to China; policy can move either way.
Supply chainDependence on TSMC and on memory supply; a disruption in Taiwan is the tail risk.

Upside: AI moving from training into everyday use (inference), robotics and sovereign AI could extend the cycle further than the model allows.

08 · Rating

Buy. The best business in tech, priced for it.

Nvidia earns more than almost any company in history and still has most of the AI accelerator market. The model values it below the price at its own cost of capital, but by less than the market as a whole is valued below its price on the same assumptions.

What would change the value: Next-year guidance pointing to growth well above 40% would raise the value; signs that cloud AI spending is peaking would lower it. Fiscal third-quarter results in November, against $108 billion of guidance, are the next test.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $180. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades above our estimated value, which is 20% below the price.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating depends on that assumption: it would be Buy if the index grows 6% a year and Hold at 10%, against 8% here. How ratings work
