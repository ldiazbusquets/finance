01 · The business

Search pays for everything else.

Google Services (79% of last quarter’s revenue) is Search, YouTube, Android, Chrome and subscriptions such as YouTube Premium and Google One. Google Cloud (21%) rents computing, including Google’s own TPU AI chips, and sells Workspace and the Gemini models to businesses. Other Bets includes Waymo.

Search still produces most of the profit. The AI question for Alphabet runs both ways: Gemini and AI Overviews have to keep Search growing while chatbots compete for the same questions, and Cloud has become one of the main ways AI labs rent computing.

Second-quarter revenue rose 24% to $119.8 billion and operating income 30% to $40.8 billion. Search grew 17%, YouTube ads 13% and Cloud 82%, with Cloud’s operating income tripling to $8.8 billion. Net income of $112.1 billion included about $98 billion of unrealized gains on the SpaceX and Anthropic stakes.

Capital spending was $44.9 billion in the quarter and free cash flow turned negative for the first time. Alphabet raised 2026 capex guidance to $195–205 billion, raised $49.6 billion in June by selling new stock ($30.5 billion of common shares and $19.1 billion of mandatory convertible preferred), issued $20.3 billion of bonds and authorized a $40 billion at-the-market share program, unused at the end of June. The shares fell 7% the next day. Third-quarter results come on October 28.

04 · Balance sheet and ownership

Selling shares to fund the build.

Alphabet’s June share sale and bond issue mark a turn for a company that had bought back stock for years. At the end of June it had $242.5 billion of cash and marketable securities and $98.2 billion of long-term debt. The marketable securities include the SpaceX stake, a listed share since the June IPO, so this model takes it out of cash to avoid counting it twice: net cash is about $50 billion. To that it adds the stakes disclosed in the June 10-Q: about 6% of SpaceX, worth $94.1 billion, and about 14% of Anthropic, about $124 billion. The stakes alone add about $18 a share, and about $22 with net cash. Most of the SpaceX stake cannot be sold until lock-ups end.

The at-the-market program will add shares; the model uses about 12.25 billion, a little above today’s count. Larry Page and Sergey Brin control the vote through Class B shares.

Hold. Search pays for the AI build, for now.

Cloud is the fastest-growing large cloud and Search is still growing in the high teens, but free cash flow has turned negative and Alphabet is selling shares to fund about $200 billion of capex this year.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $250. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades above our estimated value, which is 25% below the price.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating depends on that assumption: it would be Buy if the index grows 6% a year and Hold at 10%, against 8% here. How ratings work

Against the Street: Analysts average about $435, about 29 times consensus core earnings. This model holds the operating margin near 35% and deducts heavy net investment through 2030.
