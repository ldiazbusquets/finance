01 · The business

Half of the world’s big-jet duopoly.

Boeing Commercial Airplanes builds the 737, 767, 777 and 787; Defense, Space & Security builds military aircraft, satellites and weapons; Global Services supports both fleets with parts and maintenance, its most profitable unit. With Airbus it shares a duopoly in large passenger jets, so demand is not the problem: production is.

02 · Latest results

Record backlog, a slower ramp.

Boeing delivered 161 aircraft in the third quarter, including 124 737s, and free cash flow was $464 million after about $0.7 billion of Justice Department settlement payments. The core loss of $0.34 a share was wider than the $0.24 analysts expected, and revenue of $24.2 billion was slightly below estimates. Commercial Airplanes lost 6.5% on sales as supply-chain and rate-change costs rose; defense earned 6.2%, helped by a $252 million gain on selling HRL Laboratories, and services 18.3%. The backlog reached a record $745 billion, not yet counting the Navy’s F/A-XX fighter, and the 737-7 and 737-10 were certified.

The news was the timetable. Stabilising the 737 at 47 a month will run into 2027, with the step to 52 in the second half of 2027; the 787 move from 8 to 10 a month slipped to the end of 2026, so 787 deliveries will be at the low end of 90 to 100. Management narrowed 2026 free cash flow to about $2 billion and set about $3 billion for 2027 and $6 billion for 2028, with the $10 billion target now in 2029, about a year later than before. The 777X is still due for first delivery in 2027.

04 · Balance sheet

Heavy debt, no longer rising.

Debt was $45.9 billion against $20.7 billion of cash and investments at the end of September, with $10 billion of undrawn credit lines; debt is down from over $50 billion. Shareholders’ equity only recently turned positive after years of losses. The 2024 equity raise and asset sales were used to protect the investment-grade rating.

06 · What the price assumes

At $168, about $10 billion of cash by 2030.

The price implies free cash flow of roughly $10 billion a year by 2030, about 15% below the base case at every step, or about what Boeing now targets for 2029 with no growth after it. The market is pricing a recovery that arrives late and stops short of the pre-2019 level.

07 · Risks

What could go wrong, and what could go right.

RiskWhy it matters
Quality and the FAAAny new incident could halt the production ramp again; the 777X still needs approval for the rest of its flight tests.
Supply chainWings are the main limit on the 737, a wing-part supplier problem will add costs for several quarters, and engines pace the 787.
Customer depositsLower advance payments from airlines are one reason 2027 cash flow is guided to only $3 billion.
Fixed-price defense programsAir Force One and others have produced repeated charges.

Upside: the F/A-XX contract entering the backlog, defense growth that management now expects in the mid-single digits or higher, and the 777X entering service in 2027.

08 · Rating

Buy. A year behind plan, and priced for it.

Boeing’s backlog guarantees demand; the value depends on building planes at the planned rate and quality. The third quarter moved the $10 billion cash target from 2028 to 2029, and the price fell further than the model’s value.

What would change the value: The 737 holding 47 a month, the 787 reaching 10, 777X certification by mid-2027 and free cash flow against the $2 billion 2026 and $3 billion 2027 outlook are the inputs to watch.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $197. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades 14% below our estimated value.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating is the same whether the index is assumed to grow 6% or 10% a year. How ratings work
