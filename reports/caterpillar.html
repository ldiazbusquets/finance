01 · The business

Bulldozers, mining trucks and data-center power.

Caterpillar has three equipment segments of similar size. Construction Industries sells excavators, loaders and bulldozers. Resource Industries sells mining trucks and equipment. Power & Energy makes engines, generator sets, gas turbines (through Solar Turbines) and rail locomotives. Financial Products lends to customers and dealers.

Power & Energy is the story. Data centers need on-site generation, both as backup and, increasingly, as their main supply where the grid cannot keep up. Caterpillar’s power generation sales rose 72% last quarter, gas engine orders now stretch into late 2028 and turbine orders into 2029–2030. It is restarting a mothballed 10-megawatt gas engine line to add 1.5 gigawatts of capacity, on top of a target of 65 gigawatts by 2030.

04 · Balance sheet and ownership

Low machinery debt, steady buybacks.

Caterpillar’s balance sheet has two halves. The machinery business had $5.9 billion of cash and $11.0 billion of debt at the end of June, about $5 billion of net debt. Cat Financial carries another $35 billion of debt, matched by the loans it makes to customers, so this model leaves it out. Caterpillar bought back $1.5 billion of stock and paid $0.7 billion of dividends in the second quarter, and plans about $3.5 billion of capital spending this year.

At $704, 18% growth for two years.

Even after a 30% fall the shares trade at about 32 times forward earnings, a multiple Caterpillar has rarely held outside the bottom of a downturn. Analysts are more optimistic, with an average target of about $922, though several cut their targets this month on slowing bookings, dealer inventories and the risk that data-center power demand normalises.

08 · Rating

Hold. The AI power trade, inside a cyclical company.

Caterpillar has become one of the largest suppliers of power for AI data centers, and its backlog makes the next two years visible. About 60% of the company is still construction and mining equipment, and the price assumes about 18% growth a year for two more years.

What would change the value: Third-quarter results on October 29, especially Power & Energy orders and the backlog, are the next test.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $454. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades above our estimated value, which is 38% below the price.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating depends on that assumption: it would be Hold if the index grows 6% a year and Sell at 10%, against 8% here. How ratings work

Against the Street: Analysts average about $922 and value Caterpillar on near-term earnings at a premium multiple. This model fades growth to 3% by year ten and holds the margin near 21%, its best level so far.
