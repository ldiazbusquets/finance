01 · The business

Nuclear power, now with gas.

Constellation owns the largest nuclear fleet in America, roughly a fifth of the country’s nuclear output, and since January the Calpine fleet of gas-fired and geothermal plants. It sells power wholesale and directly to large businesses and homes. Its nuclear plants run around the clock without carbon emissions, which is what technology companies want for data centers: Microsoft signed a 20-year contract to restart the former Three Mile Island unit, and Meta one for the Clinton plant in Illinois.

Most of its earnings come from selling power at market prices or under contracts, which makes results depend on electricity prices, capacity auctions and how many long-term contracts it signs.

Second-quarter adjusted earnings rose to $2.55 a share from $1.91, mostly from Calpine; reported earnings fell to $1.42 on mark-to-market and merger costs. Constellation raised 2026 guidance to $11.50–12.50 a share from $11–12, signed 920 megawatts of new 15–20 year contracts starting in 2029–2032 (including Walmart), and is selling a Texas gas plant to LS Power for $860 million to meet the Calpine merger conditions.

The March outlook disappointed investors: base earnings growth of more than 20% in total from 2026 to 2029, slower in the first years, against expectations of much faster growth from data-center demand. That is the main reason the shares are down a third.

04 · Balance sheet and ownership

Debt tripled to buy Calpine.

At June 30 Constellation had $24.7 billion of debt and little cash, about triple a year earlier, after the Calpine deal, which also added shares (360 million diluted, from 314 million). It still bought back $2.0 billion of stock in the first half. Capital spending is well above depreciation as it uprates reactors and adds gas capacity.

08 · Rating

Hold. The best nuclear fleet, at a price still ahead of the plan.

Constellation owns the plants technology companies most want power from, and Calpine made it far larger. Its own outlook has earnings growth back-loaded toward 2029, and even after a 37% fall the price assumes margins near the model’s bull case.

What would change the value: Third-quarter results in early November, new 20-year contracts with data-center owners and capacity-auction prices are the next inputs.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $198. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades above our estimated value, which is 26% below the price.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating depends on that assumption: it would be Buy if the index grows 6% a year and Hold at 10%, against 8% here. How ratings work
