01 · The business

Rockets, moon landers and missile defense.

Firefly, based in Cedar Park, Texas, has four product lines. Alpha is a small rocket that carries about a ton to low orbit. Blue Ghost is a lunar lander: in March 2025 it made the first fully successful commercial landing on the Moon. Elytra is a spacecraft that can serve as a satellite or carry payloads around the Moon. Eclipse, a larger rocket built with Northrop Grumman, is due to fly no earlier than 2027. Its SciTec division adds defense software for missile warning, which gives Firefly a role in the Golden Dome missile-defense program.

Firefly went public in August 2025 at $45 a share, raising $868 million at a valuation of about $10 billion. In November 2026 it added a fifth line: selling its Miranda engines, and the right to build them, to AST SpaceMobile.

02 · Latest results

A record backlog, and an engine deal on top.

Third-quarter revenue rose 259% to $110.7 million, slightly below the second quarter’s $117.7 million, as spacecraft work for NASA lunar missions and defense continued. Gross margin improved to 22.9%. Net loss was $94.2 million ($0.56 a share) and free cash flow was −$85.3 million; over nine months Firefly has used $270 million of free cash flow. Backlog reached a record $1.7 billion, up from $1.5 billion in June, helped by an extension with Lockheed Martin for up to 25 Alpha launches through 2031, up to 10 launches for Synspective, and its first European launch contracts, with SSC Space in Sweden. Management raised the bottom of its 2026 revenue guidance, to $440–450 million from $420–450 million.

The bigger news came four days earlier. AST SpaceMobile agreed to buy 36 engines (27 Miranda and nine Vira, the vacuum version) for its planned heavy rocket, plus a license to build more and engineering support, in contracts valued above $1.3 billion over three years. That is more than three times this year’s revenue, and it came after the quarter, so it is not in the $1.7 billion backlog. AST will pay partly in its own shares, which Firefly cannot sell freely, and the license is worth most only if AST’s rocket works. The shares rose 37% that day, to $20.76, and closed at $23.70 on the day of the results. Alpha Block II, the upgraded rocket, is “moving toward launch”; the release gave no date for the next flight.

Alpha’s record is the weak point: a failure in April 2025 and an explosion during a ground test in September 2025 grounded it until a successful flight in March 2026. Launch is what would lift margins, so the cadence of Alpha flights matters more than any single contract. The AST deal adds a second way to earn from the same engines without having to fly them.

04 · Balance sheet and ownership

Enough cash for now, with dilution along the way.

Firefly had $535 million of cash and short-term investments at the end of September, down from $635 million in June, against $25 million of notes. During the year it repaid the $260 million drawn on its credit facility. It has sold shares twice since the IPO, including an offering at $48 in May 2026, and shares outstanding rose to 171.6 million from 159.3 million at the end of 2025. Expect more share sales before it turns profitable, though AST’s payments should help fund the engine factory. AE Industrial Partners is the largest investor; Northrop Grumman invested $50 million alongside the Eclipse partnership.

05 · Valuation

Our scenarios still give $18 a share.

With no profits yet, this report values Firefly on its 2030 revenue and the multiple of sales a profitable space company would command, discounted back at 15% a year, with more shares outstanding to fund the losses until then, and with the cash it burns until 2030 (about $0.35 billion this year, falling in a straight line to breakeven) subtracted.

This update raises 2030 revenue in the base and bull cases for the AST deal and the larger backlog, leaves the bear case alone (the deal depends on AST’s rocket), and adds five million shares to each case for the higher share count. Against that, the cash burn is heavier than the October report assumed and there is about $100 million less cash. The two roughly cancel: the base case is unchanged.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $23. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades within 10% of our estimated value.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating is the same whether the index is assumed to grow 6% or 10% a year. How ratings work

Against the Street: Analysts averaged about $38 before the third-quarter results, closer to this model’s bull case; the base case here assumes about $1.3 billion of 2030 revenue at 5× sales.
