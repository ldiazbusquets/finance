01 · The business

Submarines, tanks and Gulfstreams.

General Dynamics has four segments. Marine Systems (about a third of sales) is Electric Boat, which builds the Columbia-class ballistic-missile submarines and, with HII, the Virginia-class attack submarines. Technologies is government IT (GDIT) and mission systems. Combat Systems builds Abrams tanks, Stryker vehicles and artillery shells. Aerospace is Gulfstream business jets and their servicing.

Most sales go to the U.S. government, but Gulfstream gives it a commercial business that depends on corporate profits and wealthy buyers rather than on the defense budget.

Second-quarter revenue rose 8% to $14.1 billion and earnings 13% to $4.24 a share, ahead of forecasts. Orders of nearly $20 billion gave a book-to-bill of 1.4 and lifted backlog 32% to $136.5 billion. Gulfstream delivered 41 jets at a 14.5% margin.

General Dynamics raised 2026 guidance for the second time this year: revenue of about $55.7 billion at a 10.5% operating margin and earnings of $16.80–16.90 a share, with Marine revenue raised by almost $1.3 billion to about $18 billion. Electric Boat is on track for two Virginia-class and one Columbia-class submarine a year in the early 2030s. Third-quarter results come on October 30.

03 · The Anduril question

The least exposed of the primes.

Anduril does build for the sea: its Dive-XL and Ghost Shark autonomous submarines are designed to be built quickly and in numbers. But they complement crewed nuclear submarines rather than replace them, and no new entrant can build a Columbia-class boat. Anduril makes no tanks or business jets. The most exposed part of General Dynamics is its IT business, where software-first companies, including Anduril with its Lattice platform and Palantir, compete for the Pentagon’s data and command systems.

05 · Balance sheet and ownership

Little debt, cash conversion above 100%.

At the end of June General Dynamics had about $4.3 billion of cash and net debt of only $3.2 billion. It expects free cash flow of about 105% of net income this year, with capital spending of 3.5–4% of sales, much of it to expand submarine yards.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $349. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades within 10% of our estimated value.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating is the same whether the index is assumed to grow 6% or 10% a year. How ratings work
