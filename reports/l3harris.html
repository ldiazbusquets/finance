L3Harris

L3Harris makes satellites, secure communications, sensors and, since buying Aerojet Rocketdyne, the rocket motors inside U.S. missiles. In the third quarter about $11 billion of missile awards tripled its orders and lifted the backlog to a record $53 billion, but it also cut its 2028 cash target from $3.5 billion to about $3 billion. The shares rose 7% to $278, still well below what the model gives.

01 · The business

Space, communications and missiles.

L3Harris has three segments. Space & Mission Systems builds satellites, sensors and aircraft systems. Communications & Spectrum Dominance makes tactical radios and electronic warfare equipment, its most profitable unit. Missile Solutions makes solid rocket motors and propulsion for interceptors such as THAAD and PAC-3, a bottleneck for U.S. munitions supply.

In January 2026 the Pentagon committed a $1 billion investment in Missile Solutions ahead of a planned IPO of that business; L3Harris has since pushed the IPO to mid-2027 at the earliest and says it will only go ahead at an attractive valuation.

Third-quarter revenue rose 7% to $6.07 billion, ahead of the $5.92 billion analysts expected, and EPS of $3.28 beat the $2.95 forecast. Orders of $19.3 billion, three times revenue, took the backlog to a record $53.2 billion; about $11 billion of that was PAC-3 and THAAD interceptor work. Missile Solutions grew 17% excluding a business it sold. Free cash flow was $1.26 billion in the quarter (the company no longer counts proceeds from asset sales), and 2026 EPS guidance was raised to $12.25–12.35.

The less welcome news was in the 2028 framework. Growth of about 8% a year was kept, but the segment margin target slipped to about 16% from 16.3% and free cash flow to “$3 billion or more” from $3.5 billion, because the company will pay its 401(k) contributions in cash rather than stock, expects slightly lower margins and is spending faster on missile capacity. The shares rose 7% on the day.

04 · Balance sheet

Leveraged, but deleveraging.

Debt was about $11.0 billion at the start of October against $3.0 billion of cash and short-term investments, mostly from the Aerojet deal; the company plans to repay $800 million in the fourth quarter. It also holds $987 million from the Pentagon’s preferred-stock investment in Missile Solutions, which the model treats like debt. It pays a $1.25 quarterly dividend ($5.00 a year) and management called buybacks “highly attractive” at the current price.

05 · Valuation

The model gives $402 a share.

Because GAAP profit understates cash generation, this model works directly from free cash flow to the firm (free cash flow plus after-tax interest), following the company’s own path to 2028. This update follows the lower 2028 cash target and lowers long-run cash flow from 14.6% to 13.5% of revenue: paying the 401(k) in cash and the lower margin are lasting, while the extra missile spending should fade. Lower net debt offsets part of the cut.

08 · Rating

Buy. A good business at a discounted price.

Third-quarter orders tripled and the backlog is a record $53 billion, but the 2028 cash target was cut from $3.5 billion to about $3 billion. Even on the lower target, the price assumes far less cash than the company plans.

What would change the value: Another cut to the cash outlook, overruns on the missile ramp or new findings from the conduct investigation would lower the value.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $411. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades 43% below our estimated value.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating is the same whether the index is assumed to grow 6% or 10% a year. How ratings work
