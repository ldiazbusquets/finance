Lockheed Martin

Lockheed Martin is the world’s largest defense contractor: the F-35, the PAC-3 and THAAD missile interceptors, Sikorsky helicopters and military satellites. Its backlog just hit a record $292 billion. The question this report asks is how much of that a new kind of competitor, led by Anduril, can take.

01 · The business

Fighter jets, interceptors and satellites.

Lockheed reports four segments. Aeronautics (about 40% of sales) builds the F-35, the F-16 and the C-130. Missiles and Fire Control makes the PAC-3 and THAAD interceptors, GMLRS and PrSM rockets and long-range strike missiles. Rotary and Mission Systems is Sikorsky helicopters, radars and ship combat systems. Space builds satellites, the Orion capsule and the Trident submarine-launched missile.

About three-quarters of sales go to the U.S. government, and most of the rest to allies buying through it. Contracts run for years and are priced to give a steady single-digit to low-teens margin, which makes Lockheed a slow-growing, cash-generating business that pays out most of what it earns.

Third-quarter sales rose 11% to $20.7 billion. Missiles and Fire Control grew 24% as PAC-3, THAAD, GMLRS and PrSM production ramped, and Aeronautics 13%. Backlog jumped 27% in the quarter to $292 billion, including $52.5 billion for the PAC-3 MSE interceptor under a seven-year agreement worth up to $58.6 billion.

Lockheed raised its 2026 guidance for the second quarter running: sales of $80–82 billion, earnings of $30.35–30.75 a share and free cash flow of $7.8–8.0 billion. Management expects all four segments to grow in 2027, with a “clear line of sight” to high single-digit growth. The shares closed up about 7% on the day.

03 · The Anduril question

Where the new entrants win, and where they can’t yet.

Anduril, valued at about $90 billion in an October employee share sale, is the most visible of a group of venture-backed defense companies that build products with their own money and sell them, rather than waiting for the Pentagon to fund development. Its 2026 revenue of about $4.3 billion is roughly 5% of Lockheed’s, but it is roughly doubling every year, and it has already won where it matters most: in June 2026 the Air Force chose Anduril and General Atomics to build the first collaborative combat aircraft (drone wingmen for fighters). Lockheed, Boeing and Northrop were not picked for that hardware, though all three are in the six-company pool competing to write the autonomy software.

MarketWhere Lockheed standsHow exposed

Low-cost missiles and counter-droneIts long-range missiles are high-end; Anduril sells Barracuda cruise missiles and Roadrunner interceptorsMedium: a new low end of the market, not a replacement for the high end
Missile defense (PAC-3, THAAD)Sole producer, with seven-year agreements and $8B committed to new capacityLow for now: years of qualification and production behind it
F-35 and spaceThe only fifth-generation fighter in production for the U.S. and allies; 850 more U.S. jets plannedLow: no new entrant can build a crewed fighter or Trident this decade

The threat is real but narrow so far. Anduril’s wins come mostly in new categories the Pentagon is now funding (the 2027 request includes nearly $75 billion for unmanned and counter-drone systems), not in the programs that make up most of Lockheed’s backlog. The bigger long-term risk is budget mix: if more money goes to large numbers of cheap autonomous systems and less to high-end platforms, the primes’ share of new spending shrinks even as their old programs keep running.

05 · Balance sheet and ownership

Buybacks paused, the dividend raised again.

At the end of September Lockheed had $6.0 billion of cash and $20.5 billion of long-term debt, about $14.5 billion of net debt. It bought back no shares in the first nine months of 2026 (against $2.25 billion a year earlier) while it funds the munitions expansion and the pending purchase of Ultra Maritime, but it raised its dividend for the 24th year in a row. It expects to contribute more than $1 billion to its pension plan next year.

One item to watch: Lockheed’s costs on the F-35 Lot 20 contract have run ahead of the funding authorized so far, and the company warns that results will suffer if negotiations on Lots 20 and later do not close. Lot 20 is now expected in 2027.

09 · Rating

Buy. The incumbent with the moat, at a modest price.

Lockheed is the sole supplier of the interceptors and munitions now in most demand, under seven-year contracts. Anduril is taking the new drone and low-cost categories, which caps Lockheed’s share of future budgets, but it does not touch the F-35, PAC-3, THAAD or Trident this decade.

What would change the value: The budget after the November 3 midterms, F-35 Lot 20 funding and the 2027 guidance in January are the next inputs.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $667. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades 10% below our estimated value.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating is the same whether the index is assumed to grow 6% or 10% a year. How ratings work
