01 · The business

Bombers, ICBMs and rocket motors.

Northrop has four segments. Aeronautics builds the B-21 Raider stealth bomber and parts of the F-35. Defense Systems holds Sentinel, the new intercontinental ballistic missile, along with munitions and missile components. Mission Systems makes radars, sensors and electronic warfare systems. Space builds satellites and is one of the largest makers of solid rocket motors.

About 84% of sales go to the U.S. government. More than any other prime, Northrop’s growth depends on two programs: the B-21 and Sentinel, both strategic nuclear systems with decades of work ahead.

Third-quarter sales rose 7% to $11.1 billion, led by Aeronautics (+14%) and Defense Systems (+12%). Backlog reached a record $109.6 billion on $16.4 billion of new awards. Northrop kept 2026 sales guidance at $43.75–44.25 billion, raised segment operating income to $4.94–5.04 billion and adjusted earnings to $29.35–29.75 a share, and kept adjusted free cash flow at $3.1–3.5 billion.

For 2027 it expects sales growth of 5–7% with all four segments growing, margins up 10–20 basis points and free cash flow of $3.3–3.7 billion, with faster growth in 2028. The B-21 is meeting its test requirements, and the Air Force approved Sentinel’s design maturity, allowing a first flight in 2027.

03 · The Anduril question

Anduril is now worth more than Northrop.

At about $90 billion in its October employee share sale, Anduril is valued above Northrop’s market value of about $79 billion, on roughly a tenth of the revenue. That says more about how investors value growth than about who builds what, but the two do now compete. Northrop’s own drone fighter, the Talon Blue, flew fully autonomously this quarter, yet the Air Force picked Anduril and General Atomics, not Northrop, to build the first drone wingmen in June. Anduril also makes solid rocket motors, a business where Northrop is one of the two largest suppliers and only resumed deliveries this quarter.

Northrop is more exposed than Lockheed to the shift toward cheap autonomous systems, because its newer bets (Talon Blue, rocket motors) sit exactly where the new entrants are strongest. Its core (the B-21 and Sentinel) is about as protected as anything in defense, but it is also the part most exposed to budget politics.

05 · Balance sheet and ownership

Heavy investment, modest free cash flow.

At the end of September Northrop had $3.7 billion of cash and $15.2 billion of debt, about $11.5 billion of net debt. It is spending heavily on new factories for the B-21, Sentinel and munitions: capital spending of about $2.1 billion in 2027, or 4.5% of sales, well above Lockheed’s. That keeps free cash flow at about 8% of sales, which limits buybacks. Management says “everything is on the table” for its microelectronics and quantum businesses, including a separation.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $582. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades within 10% of our estimated value.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating is the same whether the index is assumed to grow 6% or 10% a year. How ratings work
