Nvidia

Nvidia sells the chips, networking and software that nearly every large AI model is trained and run on. Revenue more than doubled again in the third quarter and management expects about 75% growth next year. But the shares have fallen 28% in a month, on the AI-stock selloff and the sudden resignation of Jensen Huang as chief executive, and now sit below what the model supports.

01 · The business

The default supplier of AI computing.

Nvidia designs graphics processors and the systems built around them: full racks of GPUs, CPUs and networking that data centers buy as one unit, plus CUDA, the software layer most AI developers write for. It does not own factories; TSMC makes its chips.

Data centers are now almost the whole company: $107.6 billion of $115.8 billion in revenue last quarter. Its biggest customers are the cloud companies (Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Oracle, Meta) and a fast-growing group of specialist AI clouds. Several of those customers also design their own chips, which is the main long-run competitive threat alongside AMD.

On November 12 Jensen Huang, who co-founded Nvidia and ran it for 33 years, resigned as chief executive and left the board after reports about his personal conduct. Colette Kress, the finance chief since 2013, is interim chief executive while the board searches for a permanent one, and longtime director Harvey Jones became Nvidia’s first board chair.

Takeaway. The lead rests on three things competitors have to match at once: the chip, the networking that ties thousands of them together, and the software developers already use. None of them left with the founder, but the next chief executive will have to defend all three.

In the quarter to October, revenue rose 20% from the previous quarter and 103% from a year earlier, about $7 billion above the roughly $109 billion analysts expected and well above the company’s own $108 billion guidance. Earnings per share were $2.70, or $2.68 on the company’s adjusted measure against $2.47 expected. Networking passed $21 billion, and Vera Rubin, the new chip generation, brought in nearly $20 billion in its first quarter of shipments, which management called its fastest ramp ever.

Management guided the fourth quarter to $130 billion, plus or minus 3%, a quarter that includes an extra week. The guidance again assumes no data-center computing sales to China, where Nvidia sold $2.4 billion of licensed chips this quarter but has stopped shipping. Gross margin is guided down to 71–72%, which management called the bottom, recovering to 72–73% next year. It raised its outlook for next year’s revenue growth to about 75%, from 70%. Nvidia returned $28 billion through buybacks and dividends and has about $227 billion left under its buyback authorization. The shares, which closed at $155.46 before the report, rose about 4% in after-hours trading.

04 · Balance sheet

Plenty of cash, but growing commitments.

Debt is small next to a market value of nearly $4 trillion, and cash and investments roughly cover it, so the model ignores net debt. Almost all cash returns come through buybacks; the dividend is token. The bigger items sit off the balance sheet and are growing fast: purchase commitments to suppliers have reached about $390 billion, data-center lease and cloud-service commitments exceed $100 billion, and Nvidia has given about $118 billion of guarantees, most of it credit support for a single data-center campus. These would become a burden if demand fell quickly.

Read. No financial risk today; the exposure is operational and growing, through commitments and guarantees made in a boom.

05 · Valuation

The model gives $187 a share.

This update raises the base year, fiscal 2027 (to January 2027), to $424 billion: three reported quarters plus the fourth-quarter guidance. It also raises next year’s growth from 40% to 50%. Management now expects about 75%, but the model takes only part of that: the fourth quarter has an extra week, China is excluded, and more of the demand rests on customers’ borrowing and on Nvidia’s own commitments and guarantees. 06 · What the price assumes

At $155, the market prices in a sharp slowdown.

To reach today’s price, you needInstead of
Revenue growth of about 24% next year, then 15%50%, then 15%; management expects about 75%
A long-run operating margin near 44%55%; the last quarter was above 60%
A discount rate of about 14.1%12.3%

A month ago the price needed the boom to last well past 2027. Now it is consistent with growth halving from management’s outlook next year, or with margins falling to levels Nvidia has not seen since before ChatGPT. Either could happen if cloud spending turns, but neither is what the company is reporting.

07 · Risks

What could go wrong, and what could go right.

RiskWhy it matters
LeadershipHuang set the strategy and ran the customer relationships for 33 years. The search for a permanent chief executive has no timetable, and the board’s investigation is not finished.
Customer spending slowsA handful of cloud companies buy most of the output, increasingly with borrowed money. If their returns on AI disappoint, orders can fall quickly.
Commitments and guaranteesAbout $390 billion of supply commitments and $118 billion of guarantees would weigh on cash flow if demand fell.
Custom chipsGoogle, Amazon, Microsoft and Meta all design their own accelerators and would rather not pay Nvidia’s margins.
China and export rulesGuidance assumes no data-center sales to China, and new U.S. export rules released this week are still being assessed.
Supply chainDependence on TSMC and on memory supply; a disruption in Taiwan is the tail risk.

Upside: the Rubin ramp, AI moving from training into everyday use (inference), robotics and sovereign AI could extend the cycle further than the model allows; growth near management’s 75% would put the value near the bull case.

08 · Rating

Buy. The best business in tech, marked down.

Nvidia earns more than almost any company in history, and the third quarter, with revenue up 103% and a higher outlook for next year, showed demand still ahead of supply. After a 28% fall on the AI-stock selloff and its chief executive’s resignation, the model values it above the price even at its own high cost of capital, and well above the market on the same assumptions.

What would change the value: The choice of a permanent chief executive, fourth-quarter results against the $130 billion guidance and whether next year’s growth lands near the 75% management expects would move the value. Signs that cloud AI spending is peaking, or that supply commitments and guarantees are turning into losses, would lower it.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $206. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades 27% below our estimated value.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating is the same whether the index is assumed to grow 6% or 10% a year. How ratings work
