01 · The business

Keeping AI chips powered and cool.

Vertiv sells uninterruptible power supplies, switchgear and power distribution, thermal management (including liquid cooling for GPUs), and the service that keeps them running. Its customers are cloud companies, colocation operators and enterprises building data centers, now increasingly for AI.

As each rack of AI chips draws more power and generates more heat, the equipment Vertiv sells per megawatt rises. Its main competitors are Schneider Electric and Eaton.

Third-quarter sales rose 61% to $4.31 billion, 55% organically, and adjusted EPS rose 78% to $2.21, $0.41 above the company’s own guidance. Management said $100–150 million of sales that slipped out of the second quarter were delivered in this one, so part of the beat is the earlier miss catching up. Organic orders more than doubled, though Vertiv gave only the growth rate and said it will stop disclosing order figures. Adjusted operating margin was 25.2%, and adjusted free cash flow $652 million.

Full-year guidance rose again: sales of $14.45–14.85 billion, 37–39% organic growth, a 24% adjusted operating margin, adjusted EPS of $7.10–7.20 and $2.6–2.8 billion of adjusted free cash flow. The fourth quarter is guided to about $4.4 billion of sales, up about 50% organically. Management would not give 2027 numbers but expects growth well above the market. The shares rose 16% on the day.

04 · Balance sheet

From leveraged to net cash.

Vertiv was formed from a private-equity buyout and carried heavy debt. At the end of September it held $3.7 billion of cash and short-term investments against $2.9 billion of debt, about $0.8 billion of net cash, with $6.2 billion of liquidity. It keeps buying bolt-on businesses: in the quarter it announced UtilityInnovation Group (microgrids and power controls) and King Environmental Services (load testing in Europe).

08 · Rating

Sell. The execution is back. The price ran ahead of it.

Demand for Vertiv’s power and cooling equipment is not in doubt, and the third quarter, with 55% organic growth and orders more than doubled, showed it can deliver. The price rose further than the model’s value and assumes growth and margins above even the bull case.

What would change the value: Fourth-quarter organic growth against the guided 49–51%, the first 2027 guidance in February and margins above 25% are the inputs to watch.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $136. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades above our estimated value, which is 46% below the price.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating depends on that assumption: it would be Hold if the index grows 6% a year and Sell at 10%, against 8% here. How ratings work
