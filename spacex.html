01 · The business

Rockets, a satellite network and an AI cloud.

Space is the original business: Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy launches for NASA, the military and commercial customers, and Starship, the fully reusable rocket meant to cut launch costs by an order of magnitude. Connectivity is Starlink, internet from more than 9,000 satellites for homes, ships, airlines and phones. Super Intelligence (SI) came with the purchase of Elon Musk’s xAI in February 2026: the Grok models, the X platform, the Cursor coding tool, and data centers that lease computing to other AI labs.

SpaceX listed on Nasdaq as SPCX on June 12, 2026, at $135 a share, valuing it at about $1.77 trillion in the largest IPO on record.

Takeaway. SpaceX is now an AI infrastructure company with a launch business and a telecom network attached. AI is already more than half of revenue.

Starship reached orbit on Flight 14 and deployed V3 Starlink satellites. The shares rose about 10% the next day.

04 · Balance sheet and ownership

$90 billion of cash, and Musk in control.

SpaceX held $89.9 billion of cash and short-term investments against $38.8 billion of debt and leases in September, thanks to the IPO. Analysts expect debt to rise sharply as the data-center build continues.

Musk owns about 42% of the shares but about 82% of the votes through ten-vote Class B shares, so public shareholders cannot change the strategy. Insiders are locked up for a year; other pre-IPO investors can start selling around December 8, which could add supply. Musk has also said a merger with Tesla is possible but not certain.

05 · Valuation

Our scenarios give $123 a share.

A ten-year cash-flow model means little for a company whose revenue may triple in a year, so this report values SpaceX on its 2030 earning power: revenue, operating margin and a multiple, discounted back at 12% a year, plus the free cash flow between now and then. That starts deeply negative (an estimated −$68 billion this year, from nine months of reported cash flow and the guided fourth-quarter capital spending) and is assumed to rise in a straight line to 80% of 2030 after-tax profit.

The price sits between the base and bull cases: it needs the AI buildout to keep paying back as fast as management says, which is under a year per data center today.

08 · Rating

Buy. A great asset, an AI-sized bill.

SpaceX’s value now depends more on its AI data centers than on rockets or Starlink. Its free cash flow is deeply negative while it builds, which this model counts.

What would change the value: Revenue against the $33–37 billion first-quarter guidance, the pace of capital spending, and the December lock-up expiry are the next inputs.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $144. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades above our estimated value, which is 12% below the price.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating is the same whether the index is assumed to grow 6% or 10% a year. How ratings work

Against the Street: Analysts average about $223, assuming AI revenue keeps compounding near management’s guidance. The base case here reaches $500 billion of 2030 revenue, below the $660 billion the price implies.
