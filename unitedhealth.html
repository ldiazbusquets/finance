01 · The business

Insurer, doctor and pharmacy manager.

UnitedHealthcare insures tens of millions of people through employers, Medicare Advantage and Medicaid. Optum Health employs or contracts with tens of thousands of doctors; Optum Insight sells data, software and claims services to hospitals and insurers; Optum Rx manages drug benefits.

The combination is the point and the controversy: UnitedHealth earns money on both sides of many transactions, which lowers costs when it works and draws scrutiny from regulators.

Third-quarter revenue was flat at $112.7 billion as UnitedHealth shed unprofitable members, but earnings from operations rose 52% to $6.6 billion and adjusted earnings to $4.69 a share, well above forecasts. The medical care ratio, the share of premiums paid out in care, fell to 88.4% from 89.9%, helped by $610 million of favorable reserve releases. Optum Health’s profit rose to $735 million from $41 million.

Guidance was raised for the third time, to at least $20.50 a share. Medicare Advantage margins should end the year near the top of the 2–4% target and Medicaid margins return to target by the end of 2028; commercial medical costs remain above the 11% trend the company assumed.

04 · Balance sheet and ownership

Debt to capital above target.

At September 30 UnitedHealth had $74.3 billion of debt and $28.5 billion of cash and short-term investments, much of it held by regulated insurance subsidiaries. Its debt-to-capital ratio was 41.7%, above its 40% target. It bought back $5.0 billion of shares and paid $6.2 billion of dividends in the first nine months.

08 · Rating

Buy. A recovery the price only partly believes.

UnitedHealth’s margins have started to recover after the 2025 collapse, and guidance has been raised three times. The price assumes an operating margin of about 6% for good, against more than 8% before 2025.

What would change the value: The 2027 outlook, expected around the turn of the year, Medicare Advantage rates for 2028 and the outcome of federal investigations are the next inputs.

Ranges, not three cases. The expected value is the average of 4,000 runs of this report’s own model, with every input drawn from a range: the base case is the middle, and the bear and bull cases are the 10th and 90th percentiles. Business inputs move together; the discount rate moves on its own. The 25/50/25 weighting in the valuation section gives $538. How the ranges work

Two different questions. The rating is relative: it asks whether the stock is cheaper or dearer than the S&P 500 on the same assumptions. The absolute view asks whether it is worth its price at all: on its own the stock trades 22% below our estimated value.

On the same assumptions every report uses (a 5.15% Treasury yield and a 4.5% equity premium), the S&P 500 itself is worth about 68% of its price. The rating compares each stock with that: Buy if it is at least 15% cheaper than the market on this basis, Sell if at least 15% dearer, Hold in between. It is the same rule for every company, whoever owns it. The rating is the same whether the index is assumed to grow 6% or 10% a year. How ratings work
